How cyberattacks happen — 12 kinds

'Hacking' covers very different methods and harms. Each kind comes with real cases.

Ransomware

Encrypts or steals data and demands payment. Today it is often 'double extortion': steal first, then threaten to publish.

Main harm · Hospitals, factories and public services halted; threats to publish customer data

Real cases · Queens Public Library (Nov 2026) · Japanese cloud provider, 495 clients down (Oct 2026) · Asahi brewing halt (Sep 2025) · Colonial Pipeline (May 2021)

DDoS

Floods a server with traffic from many devices until the service stops.

Main harm · Banking, payment and e-government sites unreachable

Real cases · Korea: 373 DDoS reports in H1 2026, up 56.7% (MSIT/KISA) · 7.7 DDoS (2009)

Malware

Malicious software that takes over devices or servers or steals data, including web shells (server back doors).

Main harm · Data theft, server takeover, a foothold for further attacks

Real cases · PHP flaw campaign targeting 216 servers in 10 countries, 30 in Korea (reported Nov 2026)

Phishing & social engineering

Tricks people with fake emails, texts or job offers to hand over accounts and access.

Main harm · Bank accounts, corporate systems and crypto wallets compromised

Real cases · Fake résumé sites used against 3 Korean organisations (Oct 2026, Kaspersky) · social engineering in the Binance theft (Oct 2026)

Supply-chain attack

Gets into many companies at once through a supplier, outsourced service or software update.

Main harm · One breach hits dozens to thousands of client companies

Real cases · Via call-centre contractor MetaM, 22 finance and gaming firms (Nov 2026) · ~2,950 servers via a cPGuard update (Nov 2026) · 1,300+ npm package versions poisoned (Aug 2026) · SolarWinds (2020)

Industrial control systems (ICS/SCADA)

Changes the control signals and set-points of physical equipment such as water plants, refrigeration and power stations.

Main harm · Risk to drinking water, food waste, damage to medicines and blood, power cuts

Real cases · European supermarket refrigeration (Oct–Nov 2026) · French plasma freezers set to 110°C (Nov 2026) · Oldsmar water plant (2021) · Ukrainian grid (2015)

Operational-technology attack (OT)

Disables the systems that actually run factories, logistics, ports and ships.

Main harm · Production lines, ports and shipping stopped

Real cases · Jaguar Land Rover plants halted ~5 weeks (Sep 2025) · Maersk and others hit by NotPetya (2017) · LNG carrier control-system fault, cyberattack suspected (Sep 2026, under investigation)

Account & wallet theft

Takes over log-in, signing or approval steps to move money and crypto out.

Main harm · Loss of crypto and financial assets

Real cases · Binance ~$6.3bn (Oct 2026, FBI: North Korea-linked) · Bybit ~$1.5bn (Feb 2025) · Korean exchange hot wallet ~₩100m (Oct 2026)

Wiper (destructive)

Not about money: deletes data and wrecks systems so they are hard to restore.

Main harm · Loss of records, long recovery

Real cases · Russian host's cloud data wiped, 1,000+ servers checked by hand (Nov 2026) · medical-device maker's devices wiped (Mar 2026) · NotPetya (2017)

Navigation & alert spoofing

Jams or fakes GPS signals, or takes over alert apps to send false warnings.

Main harm · Ship and aircraft position errors; public fear and confusion

Real cases · Ukraine's air-alert app used to send fake radiation and chemical alerts in Kyiv (Nov 2026) · KA-SAT satellite outage cut remote monitoring of European wind turbines (Feb 2022)

Exposed APIs & side doors

Goes after the side doors — lookup services, admin tools, outsourced SaaS, forgotten files — rather than the core system.

Main harm · Mass data leaks, entry into internal networks

Real cases · 16 Korean financial firms, ~100,000 records (as of Oct 2026) · Japanese member-lookup API abused, up to ~22.5m records (Oct 2026)

AI-assisted attacks

Uses AI to automate vulnerability scanning, exploit writing and intrusion at greater speed and scale.

Main harm · Dozens to hundreds of targets at once

Real cases · Group using AI tooling arrested after stealing 8.2m+ records from ~90 Korean firms (Oct 2026) · AI agents reported to breach 100+ websites (Oct 2026)

What can be attacked remotely — the key 12

Anything connected to a network can be reached from outside. From aircraft, ships and cars to power, water and medical devices, each comes with a case that happened or was confirmed. The drawings are explanatory animations of how it works.

01

Aircraft

How it is reachedJamming or faking satellite navigation (GPS) signals puts the wrong position and time on cockpit displays. In-flight Wi-Fi and maintenance data links are other entry points.

Real caseSince 2023, far more airliners have met GPS jamming and spoofing around conflict zones such as the Baltic and the Middle East. Pilots cross-check with inertial and ground-based radio navigation.

What to doAirlines and airports: spoofing detection and backup-navigation drills. Passengers: follow official notices.

Source · GPS World

02

Ships

How it is reachedNavigation and cargo-monitoring systems linked by satellite, and the ship's AIS position beacon, are the targets. A faked AIS position makes a ship appear somewhere it is not.

Real caseIn September 2026 a Korean-owned LNG carrier lost access to some control and cargo-monitoring systems and gave up unloading off Italy (under investigation). In 2017 NotPetya halted Maersk's port and shipping systems.

What to doShipping lines: separate ship operations networks from office IT, control satellite-link accounts, train for manual navigation.

Source · The Next Web

03

Cars and EVs

How it is reachedConnected cars unlock, start and report their location through a phone app and the maker's servers. A flaw in either lets someone else send the same commands.

Real caseIn 2015 researchers remotely controlled a moving Jeep Cherokee's brakes and transmission over the mobile network, leading to a recall of 1.4 million vehicles. In 2024 a web flaw that let anyone locate, unlock and start Kia cars from the licence plate alone was found and fixed.

What to doOwners: update vehicle software, turn on two-step login for the car app, remove the previous owner's access on used cars.

Source · Wired · Sam Curry

04

Power grid

How it is reachedBreaking into the control systems (SCADA) that switch substations remotely lets an attacker open breakers and black out whole areas.

Real caseIn December 2015 attackers remotely opened substation breakers in Ukraine, cutting power to about 230,000 people — recorded as the first large blackout caused by a cyberattack.

What to doHouseholds: torches and power banks. Utilities: isolated control networks, manual-restoration drills.

Source · CISA

05

Solar inverters

How it is reachedRooftop and plant inverters connect to the maker's cloud and can be configured or switched off remotely. Many switching off at once could destabilise the grid.

Real caseIn May 2025 Reuters reported that US experts had found undocumented communication devices in Chinese-made inverters and batteries.

What to doInstallers and plant owners: audit remote access to inverters, update firmware, limit vendor-cloud links.

Source · pv magazine

06

Water plants

How it is reachedControllers that set chemical dosing and pumps at water plants can be operated from outside if they are exposed online or keep default passwords.

Real caseIn 2021 someone used remote access to try to raise the lye dose at the Oldsmar, Florida water plant more than 100-fold; an operator reversed it at once. In November 2023 several US water utilities' Unitronics controllers were breached through default passwords.

What to doHouseholds: emergency drinking water. Operators: change default passwords, take controllers off the internet, water-quality alarms.

Source · CISA · CISA

07

Medical devices

How it is reachedDevices adjusted by radio, such as pacemakers and insulin pumps, risk forged wireless commands. Hospital imaging and patient monitors also sit on the hospital network.

Real caseIn 2017 the US FDA called for a firmware update to about 465,000 Abbott (formerly St. Jude) pacemakers because of a radio vulnerability. No patient harm was reported.

What to doPatients: take the device updates your care team offers. Hospitals: keep a device inventory and segment the network.

Source · BioSpace

08

Railways

How it is reachedTrain radio and signalling are the targets. Some older radio systems trigger an emergency stop from a short, unencrypted tone sequence.

Real caseIn August 2023 someone broadcast the 'Radio-Stop' signal on Polish railway frequencies and about 20 trains halted. It took only cheap radio gear; two suspects were arrested.

What to doRail operators: move to encrypted digital radio (such as GSM-R) and monitor for rogue signals.

Source · The Record

09

Satellite links

How it is reachedSatellite internet terminals (modems) are updated remotely from a management server. If that network is breached, tens of thousands of terminals can be bricked at once.

Real caseOn 24 February 2022, the day Russia invaded Ukraine, tens of thousands of Viasat KA-SAT terminals were knocked out, and remote monitoring of about 5,800 German wind turbines was lost.

What to doOrganisations: protect terminal management accounts and keep a backup communication link.

Source · Viasat

10

Port cranes

How it is reachedContainer cranes link back to their maker for remote diagnostics and control software. Hidden communication devices raise fears of data leaks or remote shutdown.

Real caseA 2024 US House investigation found cellular modems that ports had not asked for on some cranes made by China's ZPMC. ZPMC denied posing a security threat.

What to doPorts: inspect delivered equipment for communication parts and run with remote access closed.

Source · SecurityWeek

11

Cameras, routers and home devices

How it is reachedCameras and routers left on default passwords are taken over automatically and become 'zombie' devices; hundreds of thousands of them then attack together.

Real caseIn 2016 the Mirai malware seized hundreds of thousands of cameras and routers with default passwords and used them for DDoS, knocking out DNS provider Dyn and with it access to Twitter, Netflix and other big sites.

What to doHouseholds: change default passwords, update firmware, turn off remote access you do not use.

Source · Cloudflare

12

Smartphones

How it is reachedFlaws in messaging apps can infect a phone without the user tapping anything (zero-click), exposing camera, microphone and messages.

Real caseIn 2021 forensic work by Amnesty International and others confirmed the Pegasus spyware had been installed on journalists' and activists' iPhones with zero-click exploits; Apple issued emergency security updates.

What to doIndividuals: update the OS and apps promptly, restart now and then, and use Lockdown Mode if you are at high risk.

Source · Amnesty

Rumours, checked

What is circulating online beside what officials have confirmed.

'Global blackout on 17 November'

On 17 Nov 2026 at 2:20 pm US Eastern, FEMA and the FCC run a nationwide Emergency Alert System test (announced 16 Sep): about one minute of test messages on TV and radio. It has nothing to do with cutting power, and no official source predicts a global blackout.

'All these outages are one cyberwar'

Outages clustering in time does not mean one attacker. US flights (telecom fault), Japan's shinkin banks (hardware) and Paraguay's bank (insolvency) have different causes; attacks must be confirmed case by case.

'AI will shut down power and finance on a set date'

Bill Gates (Oct 2026) and the World Economic Forum warned that AI-assisted attacks may grow — a general risk, not a forecast of a shutdown on a given date.

What to prepare — households, companies, governments

The goal is not to never be hit, but to restore essential functions quickly when an attack or outage happens.

Power

Households
Power banks, torches, emergency lights
Companies
UPS, generators, power priority for critical kit
Government
Grid restoration, backup power for the vulnerable

Comms

Paper contact list, portable radio
Companies
Fallback contact, emergency notices, phone/SMS
Government
Emergency broadcasts, redundant networks

Money

Government
Payment stability, deposit protection

Government

Emergency care, medicine supply, transfers

Security

Multi-factor login, updates, backups

Offline backups, least privilege, recovery drills

Critical-site checks, incident cooperation

Transport

Households
Alternative routes on foot or transit
Companies
Manual operation, safe-stop procedures
Government
Air, rail and port recovery plans
  1. Keep torches and power banks charged
  2. Write family contacts and meeting points on paper; keep a radio for official alerts
  3. Water, ready-to-eat food, prescriptions and hygiene items for 3 days to start
  4. Multi-factor login on phone, computer, bank and email; back up key files
  5. A little cash for outages; no panic buying or unnecessary loans
  6. Don't tap unknown links or rush 'urgent' payment requests
