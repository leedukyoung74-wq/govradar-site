How cyberattacks happen — 12 kinds

'Hacking' covers very different methods and harms. Each kind comes with real cases.

Ransomware

Encrypts or steals data and demands payment. Today it is often 'double extortion': steal first, then threaten to publish.

Main harm · Hospitals, factories and public services halted; threats to publish customer data

Real cases · Queens Public Library (Nov 2026) · Japanese cloud provider, 495 clients down (Oct 2026) · Asahi brewing halt (Sep 2025) · Colonial Pipeline (May 2021)

DDoS

Floods a server with traffic from many devices until the service stops.

Main harm · Banking, payment and e-government sites unreachable

Real cases · Korea: 373 DDoS reports in H1 2026, up 56.7% (MSIT/KISA) · 7.7 DDoS (2009)

Malware

Malicious software that takes over devices or servers or steals data, including web shells (server back doors).

Main harm · Data theft, server takeover, a foothold for further attacks

Real cases · PHP flaw campaign targeting 216 servers in 10 countries, 30 in Korea (reported Nov 2026)

Phishing & social engineering

Tricks people with fake emails, texts or job offers to hand over accounts and access.

Main harm · Bank accounts, corporate systems and crypto wallets compromised

Real cases · Fake résumé sites used against 3 Korean organisations (Oct 2026, Kaspersky) · social engineering in the Binance theft (Oct 2026)

Supply-chain attack

Gets into many companies at once through a supplier, outsourced service or software update.

Main harm · One breach hits dozens to thousands of client companies

Real cases · Via call-centre contractor MetaM, 22 finance and gaming firms (Nov 2026) · ~2,950 servers via a cPGuard update (Nov 2026) · 1,300+ npm package versions poisoned (Aug 2026) · SolarWinds (2020)

Industrial control systems (ICS/SCADA)

Changes the control signals and set-points of physical equipment such as water plants, refrigeration and power stations.

Main harm · Risk to drinking water, food waste, damage to medicines and blood, power cuts

Real cases · European supermarket refrigeration (Oct–Nov 2026) · French plasma freezers set to 110°C (Nov 2026) · Oldsmar water plant (2021) · Ukrainian grid (2015)

Operational-technology attack (OT)

Disables the systems that actually run factories, logistics, ports and ships.

Main harm · Production lines, ports and shipping stopped

Real cases · Jaguar Land Rover plants halted ~5 weeks (Sep 2025) · Maersk and others hit by NotPetya (2017) · LNG carrier control-system fault, cyberattack suspected (Sep 2026, under investigation)

Account & wallet theft

Takes over log-in, signing or approval steps to move money and crypto out.

Main harm · Loss of crypto and financial assets

Real cases · Binance ~$6.3bn (Oct 2026, FBI: North Korea-linked) · Bybit ~$1.5bn (Feb 2025) · Korean exchange hot wallet ~₩100m (Oct 2026)

Wiper (destructive)

Not about money: deletes data and wrecks systems so they are hard to restore.

Main harm · Loss of records, long recovery

Real cases · Russian host's cloud data wiped, 1,000+ servers checked by hand (Nov 2026) · medical-device maker's devices wiped (Mar 2026) · NotPetya (2017)

Navigation & alert spoofing

Jams or fakes GPS signals, or takes over alert apps to send false warnings.

Main harm · Ship and aircraft position errors; public fear and confusion

Real cases · Ukraine's air-alert app used to send fake radiation and chemical alerts in Kyiv (Nov 2026) · KA-SAT satellite outage cut remote monitoring of European wind turbines (Feb 2022)

Exposed APIs & side doors

Goes after the side doors — lookup services, admin tools, outsourced SaaS, forgotten files — rather than the core system.

Main harm · Mass data leaks, entry into internal networks

Real cases · 16 Korean financial firms, ~100,000 records (as of Oct 2026) · Japanese member-lookup API abused, up to ~22.5m records (Oct 2026)

AI-assisted attacks

Uses AI to automate vulnerability scanning, exploit writing and intrusion at greater speed and scale.

Main harm · Dozens to hundreds of targets at once

Real cases · Group using AI tooling arrested after stealing 8.2m+ records from ~90 Korean firms (Oct 2026) · AI agents reported to breach 100+ websites (Oct 2026)

Rumours, checked

What is circulating online beside what officials have confirmed.

'Global blackout on 17 November'

On 17 Nov 2026 at 2:20 pm US Eastern, FEMA and the FCC run a nationwide Emergency Alert System test (announced 16 Sep): about one minute of test messages on TV and radio. It has nothing to do with cutting power, and no official source predicts a global blackout.

'All these outages are one cyberwar'

Outages clustering in time does not mean one attacker. US flights (telecom fault), Japan's shinkin banks (hardware) and Paraguay's bank (insolvency) have different causes; attacks must be confirmed case by case.

'AI will shut down power and finance on a set date'

Bill Gates (Oct 2026) and the World Economic Forum warned that AI-assisted attacks may grow — a general risk, not a forecast of a shutdown on a given date.

What to prepare — households, companies, governments

The goal is not to never be hit, but to restore essential functions quickly when an attack or outage happens.

Households: first steps

  1. Keep torches and power banks charged
  2. Write family contacts and meeting points on paper; keep a radio for official alerts
  3. Water, ready-to-eat food, prescriptions and hygiene items for 3 days to start
  4. Multi-factor login on phone, computer, bank and email; back up key files
  5. A little cash for outages; no panic buying or unnecessary loans
  6. Don't tap unknown links or rush 'urgent' payment requests

Companies: first steps

  1. List critical services and recovery targets (RTO/RPO)
  2. Keep offline backups and test that they restore
  3. Audit every remotely reachable device, fridge controller and PLC; take them off the open internet
  4. Least privilege, multi-factor login, review contractor access
  5. Fallback procedures and drills for cloud, payment and logistics outages
  6. Patch and review side doors: lookup APIs, admin tools, forgotten files

Government: first steps

  1. Map dependencies between power, water, transport, emergency care and alerts
  2. Control links and remote access between OT and IT networks; watch for anomalies
  3. Check manual operation and emergency supply at water plants, cold stores and hospitals
  4. Regular incident-reporting, public-communication and recovery drills
